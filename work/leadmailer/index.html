This is the system I would put in front of a client with a deadline and a budget. It is the largest thing I own technically: five sending providers, roughly a million leads, revenue attribution from an affiliate network, and a live migration onto AWS.

What it does

Leadmailer takes leads from suppliers, holds them, and sends campaigns and broadcasts to those lists, then tracks opens, clicks and purchases back to the exact send that produced them. Around that core sit the parts that keep a sending operation honest: seed testing for inbox placement, sender domain management and sync, DMARC report ingestion, a compliance worker, and follow-up sends to people who have gone quiet.

Three decisions that shaped it

Keep the raw evidence, page by the last row actually seen, and never send a link you cannot attribute.

1. Every posted lead is kept, including the broken ones

Duplicates, unsubscribes and malformed records are stored rather than silently dropped. Those raw posts are the evidence behind supplier reimbursement claims, and a cleaned-up dataset is useless in that argument.

2. Counts are estimates, paging is keyset

At about a million rows and an eight second statement timeout, an exact count on the leads table gets cancelled. The symptom the operators saw was a leads page that looked empty. The fix was three things: counts maintained by database triggers, a deliberately estimated total, and paging that asks for rows after the last one it actually saw instead of using an offset. Those pages now return in roughly 0.3 to 0.5 seconds.

3. One worker per feature, attribution written into every link

Sending, resting, engagement sync, revenue sync, compliance and DMARC ingestion each run as their own worker, so they deploy and restart independently. Every outgoing link carries tracking codes added at send time, which is what makes a conversion in the affiliate network traceable back to a single email.

Scale, measured

About 1.09 million lead rows, roughly 1.02 million of them production, read from the database row estimate on 26 October 2026. Leads and seeds pages return in about 0.3 to 0.5 seconds after the query rework, measured during development the same month.

Send volume, revenue and conversion figures exist in the reporting tables and are not reproduced here: they belong to the client and they move weekly. They can be shown on a call.

The honest hard part

On 18 November 2026 a worker deploy copied a local settings file, so for about four hours and twenty minutes every worker email carried tracking and unsubscribe links pointing at localhost. Nobody reported it. It was caught because a tracked-opens count stayed at zero, and that is a monitoring gap worth stating rather than hiding.

The fix had three parts: the deploy now forces the production URL and refuses to start with a localhost value, and the links in a delivered email are checked after every deploy. Two related facts are recorded in the repository rather than glossed over: that incident, and a gap in the migration history where versions 207 to 219 were applied without being written down.

What I would do differently

I would record every migration as it is applied. Versions 207 to 219 exist in the database and not in the history, which means anyone reconstructing this schema later has to diff a live database to trust it. Deploys got a guard after the incident; the migration log should have had one from the first week.

What a reviewer can check

149 automated test files, a Playwright suite that exercises the application signed in and signed out, roughly twenty design documents covering sender deliverability, sender management, the lead-feed webhook and worker design, 242 database migrations, and a log of past mistakes. The repository is private and belongs to the client, so review happens with their permission on a call.
